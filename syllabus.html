Two sections, one course. Section 01 meets face to face on Fridays, 8:30–11:10 a.m., at the Missouri Innovation Campus (MIC); the room will be announced before classes begin. Section 02 is fully online and follows a recorded lecture of the same material. Both sections use the same modules, the same deadlines, the same exams, and the same grading standards — the difference is where you sit, not what you learn. There is no separate workshop and no discussion board.

Prerequisites

From the catalog: Prerequisite: (CS 2400 or Math 2410) and (CS 2300 or CS 4170 and Junior Status). You should be able to write and debug short Python programs; if your Python is rusty, the labs page lists free tutorials to start in week 1, and you should have seen basic data structures, probability, and matrix algebra before. Students who need a refresher on the mathematics will find a short optional primer in Module 1; it is not graded and it is enough to start the course.

Textbook and materials

  • Required: Russell and Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, 4th edition (Pearson; hardcover ISBN 978-0-13-461099-3, or the Pearson+ eText, ISBN 978-0-13-750513-5; either works). Chapters are assigned per module; the 3rd edition works for most topics with a different chapter numbering, and I will flag the differences.
  • Recommended: Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd edition — free from the authors — for Modules 6 and 7.
  • Papers: six primary research papers, read in the original, listed in the schedule. All are open access or available through the UCM library.
  • Software: Python 3.11 or newer with NumPy, and a free Google Colab or GitHub Codespaces account. No paid software is required anywhere in this course.

Description and outcomes

Catalog description: This course provides opportunities to learn the elements and techniques of artificial intelligence and how they apply to daily life. Concepts and methods are illustrated with real-world applications.

This course covers the ideas and techniques underlying the design of intelligent computer systems. We begin with the question of what makes an agent rational, then build the classical toolkit — search, constraint satisfaction, adversarial search, and decision processes — before turning to learning: reinforcement learning, probabilistic inference, supervised and deep learning, and the transformer models that now sit behind most deployed AI. The final third of the course is about judgement: how to evaluate a system honestly, how it fails, and what obligations follow from deploying it.

The course is graduate-level in its expectations rather than its prerequisites. Where an undergraduate survey would stop at “A* is optimal,” we prove it and then ask when the proof's assumptions break. Where a survey would show a neural network training, we derive the gradient, discuss what the loss function actually encodes, and examine what the reported accuracy does and does not measure.

The course is assessed by reading reflections, programming labs, two paper critiques, a midterm examination and a comprehensive final examination.

What you will be able to do

  1. Formulate a real problem as a state space, a constraint model, or a Markov decision process, and justify the formulation you chose.
  2. Select and analyze a search or optimization algorithm using completeness, optimality, time and space complexity, and the properties of the heuristic you supply.
  3. Derive and implement value iteration, policy iteration, temporal-difference learning, and policy-gradient methods, and explain the assumptions each one makes.
  4. Represent uncertain knowledge as a Bayesian network, reason about conditional independence, and perform exact and approximate inference.
  5. Train, regularize, and diagnose classical and deep models, and report performance with uncertainty rather than a single number.
  6. Explain how transformer language models are built and adapted, and design an evaluation that would actually detect the failures you care about.
  7. Read a research paper in the field critically: identify its claim, its evidence, its baselines, and the conditions under which the claim would not hold.
  8. Assess an AI system for safety, fairness, privacy, and legal exposure, and write a recommendation a non-technical decision maker could act on.

How a week works, in either section

The rhythm is the same every week and in both sections, so you can plan a term around it. The only differences between Section 01 and Section 02 are whether you attend the Friday class in person or follow the recorded lecture, and how the two exams are delivered.

What differs between the two sections. Everything this table does not list is identical.

A student in either section may use the other section's format when it helps: a face-to-face student who is travelling can follow the recorded lecture, and an online student who happens to be near MIC can join the Friday class. Tell me in advance.

  1. 1
    Monday: the week opens

    The week's slides, the reading, and the week's lab or reflection appear together on the course home. Budget six to eight hours of study across the week for a 3-credit graduate course.

  2. 2
    Friday, 8:30–11:10 a.m. Central: class

    The lecture, a worked problem, and open questions. Section 01 meets at MIC; Section 02 watches the recorded lecture of the same material during the week. Weeks 3, 6, 10 and 13 also have a playground: exercises that run in your browser and check your answers.

  3. 3
    Sunday, 11:59 p.m. Central: the week's work is due

    A lab or a reading reflection, and twice in the term a paper critique, submitted in Brightspace. Labs 2–5 are due about nine days after the Friday class that teaches them; the schedule gives every date. Written work is returned within seven days.

Week 8 is the one exception. There is no lecture that week and no new reading: the midterm examination replaces the module. The exam briefing has the scope, format, sample questions, and the arrangements for each section.

Grade scale

A 90–100 · B 80–89 · C 70–79 · D 60–69 · F below 60, the scale in the department's master syllabus. Graduate credit in the School of Computer Science requires a grade of B or better in each course counted toward the degree; a C earns credit hours but may trigger a program review, and it does not satisfy a required course.

Late work

Late labs, reflections and critiques lose 10% per day, for up to three days. After three days they are not accepted. There are no free late days. The two exams have fixed dates; see below.

Labs

Labs are individual work, except lab 6, where pairs may build the system together and each partner submits an individual report. You may discuss approaches with classmates, and you must cite any code, derivation, or dataset you did not write yourself. Each lab includes a written report of four to seven pages, worth between a fifth and a third of the lab grade, depending on the lab; each lab's rubric states the split. A working implementation that never explains its results tops out in the C range.

Examinations

  • Midterm: Friday, March 5, 8:30–10:30 a.m. CT, modules 1–7, 100 points. The week 8 briefing, the practice midterm and the midterm formula sheet show the format.
  • Final: Friday, May 7, 8:30–10:30 a.m. CT, in finals week, the whole course weighted to modules 9–15, 100 points. The final exam page has the parts and the formula sheet, and the sample final, with answers, shows the format. The real exams ask different questions from the practice and sample exams.
  • Both exams are closed book. Bring a pen and nothing else: no notes or other paper, calculators, phones or AI assistants. The last page of each exam is a one-page formula sheet with every formula the exam needs, the same sheet linked above.
  • Online section: you take each exam in Brightspace with Honorlock, with your camera on and your screen recorded. The quiz opens when Section 01's sitting ends and closes that Sunday at 11:59 p.m. CT: Friday, March 5, at 10:30 a.m. to Sunday, March 7, for the midterm and Friday, May 7, at 10:30 a.m. to Sunday, May 9, for the final. Take it once in that window, with the same 120-minute limit. The formula sheet is attached to the quiz.
  • If illness or an emergency makes an exam date impossible, tell me before the exam if you possibly can. A make-up is given for a documented reason; an incomplete grade is possible where the situation warrants one.

Course policies

Academic integrity

Collaboration on understanding is encouraged; collaboration on submitted work is not, except where an assignment says otherwise. Presenting another person's code or writing as your own, reusing a submission from another course, or fabricating results is a violation of the UCM academic honesty policy and will be handled according to it, which can include a failing grade for the course. If you are unsure whether a particular form of help is allowed, ask before you submit rather than after.

Attendance

Section 01 is expected to attend the Friday class, and Section 02 to watch each week's recorded lecture before that week's work is due. Attendance is not graded, but the exams assume everything shown in class. If you miss a class, watch its recording; if you will miss an exam, see Examinations.

Communication

There is no discussion board in this course. Questions about the material are best asked in class or office hours; everything else, including accommodations, illness, extensions and grading questions, goes to me by email, and I reply within one business day at kar@ucmo.edu. My office phone is (660) 543-6612. Office hours are Tuesday and Wednesday, 12:00–3:00 p.m., in MIC D159, and by appointment.

Recording and privacy

Lecture recordings are provided for your own study and may not be redistributed. The recorded lecture for Section 02 is made separately in my office, so the Friday class and the questions asked in it are not recorded. Student audio and video are never recorded without explicit permission.

Religious observance and university activities

If a religious observance or a documented university activity conflicts with a deadline, tell me within the first two weeks of the term and we will arrange an equivalent alternative.

Policy on AI tools

This course follows the department's limited use model for generative AI.

You are learning to build these systems, so a blanket ban would be both unenforceable and intellectually dishonest. The rule is about disclosure and about what you can defend, not about which tool you opened.

What is permitted, by task type.
Work Generative AI allowed? What you must do
Reading reflections and paper critiques For brainstorming and grammar only Disclose use in a one-line note. The argument and the judgement must be yours.
Programming labs For debugging help and explaining errors Disclose the tool and the prompt topic. You must be able to explain every line you submit.
Midterm and final examinations No Closed book, no assistants. Using one is an integrity violation.

Two things follow, and I will hold to both. First, an unacknowledged use is an integrity violation regardless of how small the use was. Second, if you cannot explain your submission without the tool in front of you, you have not done the assignment — and in an oral check I may ask you to do exactly that.
