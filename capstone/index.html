What this project is

The capstone is an AI system, an analysis of one, or a rigorous replication — something where a defensible answer is possible in eight weeks and where the interesting part is not the accuracy number. Every project must contain four things, and a project missing any of them is not finished regardless of how well the code runs:

  1. A claim that could be false. "Build a chatbot for the library" is a task. "A retrieval-augmented assistant over library policy documents reduces wrong-answer rate below the plain model's, at the cost of more refusals" is a claim — it can fail, and the failure would teach something.
  2. An evaluation that could refute it. A baseline, a metric, a held-out set, and a stated condition under which you would conclude you were wrong.
  3. A failure case, examined. One concrete input where the system fails, with an explanation of why and what it implies about the design.
  4. A model card. The system's intended use, limits, evaluation data, and known failure modes, written for someone deciding whether to trust it.

What it is not: a literature review with a demo attached, a wrapper around an API with no measurement, or an attempt to train something large enough to need hardware you do not have. All three have been submitted before, and all three score badly.

Three shapes that work

Pick one. Each is graded the same way: claim, evidence, failure, card.
Shape What you do What "finished" looks like
Build and evaluate Implement a system, then design an evaluation that could falsify your own claim about it. Example: a question-answering system over UCM course catalogs, measuring whether retrieval or fine-tuning drives the improvement. An ablation that isolates the thing you claimed mattered, against a computed baseline, with the cost of the improvement stated.
Analyze an existing system Take a deployed model or dataset and characterise where it breaks. Example: how a public recommendation model's error rate varies across demographic subgroups and across time. A measurement study with a pre-registered slice, not a hunt for the worst example you can find after the fact.
Replicate and stress-test Reproduce a published result, then vary one assumption. Example: replicate a policy-gradient result and test its sensitivity to the seed budget the paper reported. A reproduction table next to the paper's own numbers, then a one-variable sensitivity study that explains any gap.

The week-5 test

Before the proposal is due, write your project as one sentence of this form and see whether it survives being read aloud:

I claim that [intervention] improves [metric] over [baseline] on [data], and I will know I am wrong if [specific condition].

If you cannot fill all four brackets, the scope is wrong — usually too large. Bring the sentence to office hours in week 5; that conversation is free and it is much cheaper than a failed checkpoint. Every term, the projects that struggle are the ones that tried to train a large model from scratch on a laptop. A small, carefully evaluated system beats an ambitious one you cannot finish.

The checkpoint (5%)

The checkpoint is not a progress report; it is evidence that the pipeline runs end to end on real data. Fill in the checkpoint template: one page with four headings.

  • Pipeline status — the command that reproduces your current result, and what it consumes.
  • Preliminary results — one table or figure, clearly labelled as preliminary. Numbers that will change are fine; numbers you have not produced are not.
  • Risks now — what is most likely to stop you, and the fallback you will take if it does.
  • Changes since the proposal — what you narrowed, and why. Scope reduction here is normal and costs nothing; scope reduction in May costs marks.

Then a ten-minute meeting, booked from the sign-up sheet, in weeks 9–10. Bring the pipeline runnable. If the demo does not run in the meeting, the checkpoint is incomplete regardless of the document.

The final report (8–12 pages, 12%)

Two-column conference format, eight to twelve pages including references, figures and tables. Use IEEE or NeurIPS style and stay consistent; both are fine and no credit is awarded for the choice. Single column is acceptable only with a written reason. The page limit excludes the model card appendix.

The failure case (required)

One page, in the report, on a single input where your system is wrong. Choose it honestly — the most interesting failure, not the least embarrassing one. Answer four questions:

  1. What was the input, and what did the system do?
  2. What should it have done, and how do you know?
  3. Why did it fail — data, formulation, or implementation? Be specific enough that the answer rules something out.
  4. What would you change, and what would that change cost elsewhere?

A report with no failure case is incomplete. A report whose failure case is "sometimes the model is wrong" earns nothing for that section.

The model card (required appendix)

Fill in the model card template. It is the document someone reads to decide whether to deploy your system, so it must be written for them rather than for your grader: intended use, out-of-scope use, evaluation data, metrics with their variance, and known failure modes. For a replication or an analysis rather than a built system, the card describes the system you studied and what your analysis establishes about it.

The presentation (12 minutes + questions, 8%)

Twelve minutes, then up to eight minutes of questions. A demo is expected: live if it is reliable, recorded if it is not — a recording is not a penalty, a live failure is not a story. Structure that works: two minutes of question and why it matters, seven of method and results, two of the failure case, one of what you would do next.

Rules

Individual and pair work

You may work alone or in a pair. Pairs submit one report and one presentation, and both partners must be able to defend every part of it in questions. Pairs also submit a half-page contribution statement with the final report, naming who did what; a pair whose statement describes one person doing the work should expect to be asked about it.

AI tools

Permitted as tools in the work itself, documented as you would document a library: name the model and version, say what it was used for, and describe how you checked its output. Not permitted to write your analysis, your failure case, or your limitations section. You must be able to explain every line you submit.

Data, people, and permission

Use data you are allowed to use. Public datasets are fine; a scraped site is fine only if its terms allow it, and you must say so in the report. If your project involves human subjects — surveys, interviews, user studies, or identifiable records — it requires IRB review before you collect anything, and that takes weeks, not days. Ask in week 5 if you are unsure; a project that turns out to need IRB approval in April cannot be finished.

Late work and fixed dates

The proposal and checkpoint follow the syllabus late policy: 10% off per day for up to three days, then not accepted. The final report and the presentation have fixed dates: if something makes them impossible, contact me before the deadline and we will find an arrangement, including an incomplete grade where the situation warrants one.

Checklist before each deadline

  • Proposal: can a reader state your claim back to you after one reading, and does your evaluation plan name a baseline and a falsification condition?
  • Checkpoint: does your reproduction command run from a clean checkout, and is there a number in your document that came from real data?
  • Report: is the failure case a specific input, and does the model card say who should not use the system?
  • Presentation: is your claim on screen before the first slide change, and does the demo run twice in a row on the machine you will use?
