1. Question and claim

One sentence in the form below, then a paragraph of context. The sentence is the whole proposal in miniature; write it first and let the rest support it.

I claim that __________ improves __________ over __________ on __________, and I will know I am wrong if __________.

Context, 150–200 words: what the problem is, who has it, and why the answer is not already obvious. Cite two sources.

2. Why it matters

Two sentences. What changes in practice, or in how someone should read existing work, if your claim holds? What changes if it fails? A claim whose failure changes nothing is not worth eight weeks.

3. Method

250–350 words. What you will build or measure, in enough detail that a reader could object to a specific choice. Name the components, the data flow, and the two decisions you expect to be hardest. A reader should be able to say "that choice is wrong" and point at it.

4. Evaluation plan

The most important page of the proposal. Fill every cell; "TBD" in the baseline or falsification row is the single most common reason a proposal loses marks.

ElementYour plan
BaselineWhat you compare against, and why it is the strongest simple thing — not the weakest
MetricWhat you measure, in what units, and why that metric rather than accuracy
Data and splitWhat data, how much, and how the held-out set stays held out
VarianceHow many seeds or repeats, and what you will report alongside the mean
FalsificationThe result that would make you conclude you were wrong
CostWhat your improvement costs — time, compute, refusals, latency
