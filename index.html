Two sections, one course. Section 01 meets face to face on Fridays, 8:30–11:10 a.m., at the Missouri Innovation Campus (MIC); the room will be announced before classes begin. Section 02 is fully online and follows a recorded lecture of the same material. Both sections use the same modules, the same deadlines, the same exams, and the same grading standards — the difference is where you sit, not what you learn. There is no separate workshop.

Prerequisites

Graduate standing in computer science, data science, or a related program, or consent of the instructor. You should be able to write and debug short Python programs; if your Python is rusty, the labs page lists free tutorials to start in week 1, and you should have seen basic data structures, probability, and matrix algebra before. Students who need a refresher on the mathematics will find a short optional primer in Module 1; it is not graded and it is enough to start the course.

Textbook and materials

  • Required: Russell and Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, 4th edition. Chapters are assigned per module; the 3rd edition works for most topics with a different chapter numbering, and I will flag the differences.
  • Recommended: Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd edition — free from the authors — for Modules 6 through 8.
  • Papers: six primary research papers, read in the original, listed in the schedule. All are open access or available through the UCM library.
  • Software: Python 3.11 or newer with NumPy, and a free Google Colab or GitHub Codespaces account. No paid software is required anywhere in this course.

Description and outcomes

This course covers the ideas and techniques underlying the design of intelligent computer systems. We begin with the question of what makes an agent rational, then build the classical toolkit — search, constraint satisfaction, adversarial search, and decision processes — before turning to learning: reinforcement learning, probabilistic inference, supervised and deep learning, and the transformer models that now sit behind most deployed AI. The final third of the course is about judgement: how to evaluate a system honestly, how it fails, and what obligations follow from deploying it.

The course is graduate-level in its expectations rather than its prerequisites. Where an undergraduate survey would stop at “A* is optimal,” we prove it and then ask when the proof's assumptions break. Where a survey would show a neural network training, we derive the gradient, discuss what the loss function actually encodes, and examine what the reported accuracy does and does not measure.

What you will be able to do

  1. Formulate a real problem as a state space, a constraint model, or a Markov decision process, and justify the formulation you chose.
  2. Select and analyze a search or optimization algorithm using completeness, optimality, time and space complexity, and the properties of the heuristic you supply.
  3. Derive and implement value iteration, policy iteration, temporal-difference learning, and policy-gradient methods, and explain the assumptions each one makes.
  4. Represent uncertain knowledge as a Bayesian network, reason about conditional independence, and perform exact and approximate inference.
  5. Train, regularize, and diagnose classical and deep models, and report performance with uncertainty rather than a single number.
  6. Explain how transformer language models are built and adapted, and design an evaluation that would actually detect the failures you care about.
  7. Read a research paper in the field critically: identify its claim, its evidence, its baselines, and the conditions under which the claim would not hold.
  8. Assess an AI system for safety, fairness, privacy, and legal exposure, and write a recommendation a non-technical decision maker could act on.

Grade scale

A 90–100 · B 80–89 · C 70–79 · F below 70. Plus and minus grades are assigned at the instructor's discretion. Graduate credit in the School of Computer Science requires a grade of B or better in each course counted toward the degree; a C earns credit hours but may trigger a program review, and it does not satisfy a required course.

Late work

Late work on any lab, reflection, critique, or on the capstone proposal or checkpoint loses 10% per day, for up to three days. After three days it is not accepted. The midterm, the capstone report, and the capstone presentation have fixed dates; if something makes those dates impossible, contact me before the deadline and we will find a workable arrangement, including an incomplete grade where the situation warrants one.

Labs

Labs are individual work, except lab 6, where pairs may build the system together and each partner submits an individual report. You may discuss approaches with classmates, and you must cite any code, derivation, or dataset you did not write yourself. Each lab includes a written report of four to seven pages, worth between a fifth and a third of the lab grade, depending on the lab; each lab's rubric states the split. A working implementation that never explains its results tops out in the C range.

Capstone project

The capstone is the one piece of work in this course where you choose the question. It should be an AI system, an analysis of one, or a rigorous replication — something where a defensible answer is possible within eight weeks and where the interesting part is not the accuracy number.

Strong projects in past terms have taken one of three shapes:

  • Build and evaluate. Implement a system, then design an evaluation that could falsify your own claim. Example: train a small language model to answer questions over UCM course catalogs and measure whether retrieval or fine-tuning drives the improvement.
  • Analyze an existing system. Take a deployed model or dataset and characterize where it breaks. Example: measure how a public recommendation model's error rate changes across demographic subgroups and across time.
  • Replicate and stress-test. Reproduce a published result, then vary one assumption. Example: replicate a policy-gradient result and test how sensitive it is to the random seed budget reported in the paper.

Course policies

Academic integrity

Collaboration on understanding is encouraged; collaboration on submitted work is not, except where an assignment says otherwise. Presenting another person's code or writing as your own, reusing a submission from another course, or fabricating results is a violation of the UCM academic honesty policy and will be handled according to it, which can include a failing grade for the course. If you are unsure whether a particular form of help is allowed, ask before you submit rather than after.

Communication

Questions that would help a classmate belong in the course discussion board, where everyone benefits from the answer. Personal matters — accommodations, illness, extensions, grading questions — go to me by email, and I reply within one business day at kar@ucmo.edu. My office phone is (660) 543-6612. Office hours are Tuesday and Wednesday, 12:00–3:00 p.m., in MIC D159, and by appointment.

Recording and privacy

Lecture recordings are provided for your own study and may not be redistributed. The recorded lecture for Section 02 is made separately in my office, so the Friday class and the questions asked in it are not recorded. Student audio and video are never recorded without explicit permission.

Religious observance and university activities

If a religious observance or a documented university activity conflicts with a deadline, tell me within the first two weeks of the term and we will arrange an equivalent alternative.

Policy on AI tools

You are learning to build these systems, so a blanket ban would be both unenforceable and intellectually dishonest. The rule is about disclosure and about what you can defend, not about which tool you opened.

What is permitted, by task type.
Work Generative AI allowed? What you must do

Second, if you cannot explain your submission without the tool in front of you, you have not done the assignment — and in an oral check I may ask you to do exactly that.

Accessibility and accommodations

This course is built to meet the accessibility standard the university is obligated to meet under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, version 2.1, level AA. The standard applies to both sections, and both sections use the same material files, so an accommodation arranged for one section works in the other. That is a floor, not an achievement, and I would rather hear about a barrier than have you work around one.

What is already in place

  • Every slide is real text, not an image of text, so it reads correctly with a screen reader and reflows at 200% zoom.
  • Every diagram carries a written description and a caption that states its point, not just its title.
  • Decks are fully keyboard operable: arrow keys move between slides, and slide changes are announced.
  • Colour is never the only signal; every highlighted element is also labelled.
  • Body text meets a 4.5:1 contrast ratio against its background, and the course uses a white background throughout.
  • All video is captioned and every recorded lecture has a transcript within five business days.
  • Documents are provided in their original formats so you can enlarge, recolour, or convert them.

Requesting an accommodation

Accommodations are arranged through the Office of Accessibility Services, which will send me an accommodation letter. Contact them as early in the term as you can; accommodations are not retroactive, but I will work with you on anything that has already passed. You do not need to disclose a diagnosis to me, and you never need to justify the request in class.

If something is not accessible

Tell me which material and what happened — a diagram a screen reader cannot describe, a video without captions, a PDF that will not reflow. I will provide an accessible alternative, normally within two business days, and fix the source file so the next student does not hit it. You can also raise it with Accessibility Services if you prefer not to come to me first.

Printing and PowerPoint. Each lecture deck prints to a 16:9 PDF directly from the browser: open the deck, press P or use your browser's print command, and choose “Save as PDF.” The page is already sized to 13.333 × 7.5 inches, one slide per page, with no browser headers or footers if you turn them off in the print dialog.
