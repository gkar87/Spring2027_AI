Laboratories

Six labs, six ideas you cannot learn by reading. Each is worth 4% of the course grade, and each has a written report that carries a fifth to a third of the marks.

The sequence

The labs follow the lecture order deliberately. Each one reuses code from the last: lab 3 learns the policy lab 2 computed, lab 6 evaluates the kind of system lab 5 taught you to judge. Keep your code in a state you would be willing to open again.

Every dataset, map, network and corpus these sheets refer to is in labs/data. The files are fixed for the semester, so any number you report can be regenerated from them.

Together the labs are 24% of the grade. The written part of each — a fifth to a third of that lab's marks — is not optional padding: an implementation that works and a report that says nothing earns a C.

What every lab asks for

  • A reproduction command. One command, from a clean checkout, that regenerates every number in your report. Numbers without this are not believed.
  • Numbers, not adjectives. "Faster" is not a result; "expanded 412 nodes instead of 1,180" is.
  • Honest comparisons. Baselines first, more than one seed where randomness is involved, and no tuning against the data you report on.
  • A written report. Four to seven pages, written for a colleague who has to make the same decision you made.

How the labs are graded

Each lab sheet ends with a what the grader runs section: the exact command the grader calls, and the one JSON object it expects on stdout. Match that interface exactly, and print nothing else to stdout.

The grader checks outputs, never your source: lab 1 against the reference table in data/lab1, lab 2 by checking that your values satisfy the Bellman equation, lab 3 against the range real runs produce, lab 4 against exact inference, lab 5 by scoring your predictions file, and lab 6 by scoring your answers against the key. Each sheet tells you how to make the same check yourself before you submit.

Submissions are also compared with each other for similarity. That comparison is triage, not a verdict: a flag means two submissions get read side by side, and you will be asked about your code rather than accused by a script.

New to Python? Start here

The labs assume you can read and write short Python programs. If that is new, spend an hour or two on the first two links before lab 1, and keep the rest open while you work. Each lab sheet also says which of these it leans on.

  • The official Python tutorial: variables, lists, dictionaries, functions and classes, chapters 3 to 9.
  • Python for Everybody: a free beginner course with short videos and exercises.
  • Lists, dictionaries and sets, the structures every lab uses.
  • heapq and collections: priority queues and deques for lab 1.
  • json and argparse: reading the data files and building the command line every grader calls.
  • NumPy for absolute beginners: arrays, for labs 2 to 5.
  • Python and NumPy tutorial (Stanford CS231n): a fast refresher that covers both.
  • Pyplot tutorial: the figures every report needs.
  • Learn the basics (PyTorch): for lab 5.
  • Hugging Face LLM course and Sentence Transformers quickstart: for the dense retriever in lab 6.

Office hours are for exactly this kind of question: Tuesday and Wednesday, 12 to 3 p.m., MIC D159.

Policies that apply to all six

The syllabus late policy applies throughout: 10% off for each day late, for up to three days. After three days a lab is not accepted. AI assistants are permitted for boilerplate, plotting and debugging, and must be disclosed — the analysis and the writing are yours. Undisclosed generated work is an academic integrity matter under the university policy referenced in the syllabus.

Every lab has a check-yourself list at the end. It is not a rubric, but if you cannot answer those questions about your own submission, neither can the grader.
