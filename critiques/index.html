Paper critiques

Two papers, one two-page critique each. Ten percent of the course, and the only place where you are asked to decide whether a published result deserves to be believed.

How to read a paper in ninety minutes

  1. Ten minutes: the frame. Title, abstract, figures, conclusion. Write the claim in one sentence before reading anything else.
  2. Twenty minutes: the evidence. Which table or figure would be the problem if the claim were false? Read only those, and check what is being compared with what.
  3. Thirty minutes: the method. Enough to know what was held constant. Note every choice a reader could dispute — dataset, baseline, metric, hyperparameters, evaluation protocol.
  4. Twenty minutes: the related work and the claims. What does the paper say others failed to do, and is that characterisation fair?
  5. Ten minutes: your objection. Write the strongest objection you can, then write the response the authors would give.

What a critique contains

Two pages, five sections, in this order. The word counts are guidance.

SectionWordsWhat it must do
1. The claim100 State the paper's central claim precisely, and the claim it argues against. Not the topic — the claim.
2. The evidence250 Name the specific experiment, table or figure that carries the claim, and say what it establishes and what it does not.
3. The load-bearing assumption200 The assumption that, if wrong, collapses the result. Say why it is plausible or not, using something you know.
4. The experiment that would settle it150 One experiment the authors did not run, with what it would show either way. It must be feasible — not "train a bigger model".
5. What the paper does not establish100 One sentence a careless reader would take away that the paper does not support.

Critique 1 — questions for Vaswani et al.

Answer these inside the five sections; they are the ones I will mark against.

  1. The paper's title is a claim of sufficiency, not just of performance. What does the ablation table actually show about recurrence being unnecessary?

  2. Self-attention has no notion of order. How is position supplied, and what evidence is offered that the choice of positional encoding does not matter? Is that evidence adequate?

  3. The complexity comparison in the paper's Table 1 is asymptotic. What would a fair comparison at the sequence lengths in the experiments look like, and does the paper's own data support the asymptotic argument at those lengths?

  4. Which baseline is the fairest one in the translation results, and what happens to the headline improvement when you compare against it rather than against the paper's own previous system?

  5. Name the one experiment you would run to test whether the gain comes from attention itself or from the training regime that became possible with it.

Critique 2 — questions for Ouyang et al.

  1. The headline evaluation is human preference between two outputs. What exactly does a preference rate measure, and what does it fail to measure?

  2. The reward model is trained on labels from the same pool of labellers who later judge the outputs. What does that shared provenance do to the interpretation of the result?

  3. The paper reports both an automated metric and human preference on the same task. Where do they disagree, and what does the disagreement tell you about which one to trust?

  4. The alignment tax — capability lost to the preference training — is reported on a limited set of tasks. How would you measure it more convincingly?

  5. What does the paper establish about safety, as distinct from helpfulness, and what does it explicitly leave open?

How critiques are marked

Each critique is worth 5%. Sections 3 and 4 carry half of it.
CriterionWeightFull marks
Accuracy of the claim25% The claim is stated as the paper states it, including its scope, and the target of the argument is named
Reading of the evidence25% Points at the specific experiment that carries the claim, and separates what it shows from what it is taken to show
The assumption25% Identifies an assumption that genuinely bears the weight, and argues about it rather than gesturing
The proposed experiment25% Feasible, specific, and its possible outcomes are stated in advance

A critique that dislikes the paper without engaging its evidence scores lower than one that accepts the result and shows precisely where it stops being supported. Being right is not required; being specific is.

Rules

Two pages, single-spaced, your own words, submitted as PDF. Cite the paper by section, table or figure number — "the paper says" is not a citation. The syllabus late policy applies: 10% off per day for up to three days, then not accepted. AI assistants may help you find background or check a definition; they may not summarise the paper for you, and a critique of a summary you did not read is usually detectable and always worthless.

Discussion with classmates is encouraged before you write. Critique 1 is returned with comments in week 11; use them. Critique 2 is marked against the same rubric and students who act on the first set of comments reliably score higher.

Check yourself before submitting

  • Have you named the claim, rather than the topic?
  • Does your evidence section point at one specific table or figure?
  • If your load-bearing assumption were false, would the result actually collapse?
  • Is your proposed experiment one a graduate student could run in a week?
  • Does your last section name something a careless reader would wrongly conclude?
