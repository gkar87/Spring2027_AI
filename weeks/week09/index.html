Probability you need

The joint distribution and three rules.

The joint distribution is the whole story, and it is unusable

With n binary variables, the joint distribution has 2n − 1 independent numbers. For 30 variables that is about a billion.

Every query you could ask — P(X | Y = y), a most-likely explanation, a decision — is a sum over that table. The table is correct and completely impractical.

2³⁰ − 1 ≈ 1.07 × 10⁹ parameters

The fix is structure, again

Most variables are conditionally independent of most others. A Bayesian network records exactly which direct dependencies exist, and the joint factors into a product of small local terms.

In the classic alarm network, 5 variables need 10 numbers instead of 31. At 30 variables the saving is astronomical.

The three rules you need

Product rule

P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b)

Applied repeatedly, it gives the chain rule for any ordering of variables.

Marginalization

P(a) = Σb P(a, b)

Sum out what you do not care about. This is where inference gets expensive.

Bayes' rule

P(a | b) = P(b | a) P(a) / P(b)

Turn a causal probability into a diagnostic one — the direction you usually need.

Why Bayes' rule matters

A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. The disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. You test positive. What is the probability you have it?

P(D|+) = (0.99 × 0.0001) / (0.99 × 0.0001 + 0.01 × 0.9999) ≈ 0.0098

About 1%, not 99%. The base rate dominates, and this is the single most common reasoning error in applied AI — including in classifiers with impressive precision on rare events.

Independence is rare; conditional independence is everywhere

Independence

A and B are independent if P(A, B) = P(A)P(B). Knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

Conditional independence

A and B are conditionally independent given C if P(A, B | C) = P(A | C) P(B | C).

Neither implies the other

Dependent, but independent given a cause. A student's grade in two courses is correlated — until you condition on the underlying ability.

Independent, but dependent given a common effect. Two coins are independent. Condition on "at least one is heads" and they become dependent: if the first is tails, the second must be heads.

§

That second example is explaining away, and it is the pattern people find hardest to accept. It is also a d-separation case.

Bayesian networks

A factorization you can draw.

A Bayesian network is a factorization with a picture

A directed acyclic graph whose nodes are random variables. Each node has a conditional distribution given its parents. The network defines the joint:

P(x₁, …, xn) = ∏i P(xi | parents(Xi))

An edge means "direct influence". The absence of an edge is a strong claim: no direct dependence, given the parents.

The graph must be acyclic. Cycles would make the factorization circular and the distributions ill-defined.

The general rule

A node with k parents needs 2k numbers (for binary variables). Total cost is exponential in the in-degree, not the number of variables.

cost ≈ n · 2k

This is why sparse networks are tractable and dense ones are not. A node with 20 parents costs a million numbers whether the network has 21 nodes or 2,000.

§

The compactness is not a convenience. It is what makes the model learnable: 31 numbers need 31 observations in the worst case, and 10 need far fewer.

A query, computed

P(B | j, m): given that both John and Mary call, how likely is a burglary? By Bayes' rule and marginalization:

P(B | j, m) = α P(B) Σe P(e) Σa P(a|B,e) P(j|a) P(m|a)

The network made this factorable. Without it, you would need P(B, e, a, j, m) for every combination.

What the answer means

Two independent calls still leave a 72% chance of no burglary, because burglaries are rare and false alarms are common. The evidence raises the probability from 0.001 to 0.284 — a factor of 284 — and it is still unlikely.

Strong evidence and a high probability are different claims.

This is the base-rate lesson from the Bayes' rule slide again, in a network. It will return in module 11 as precision and recall.

Reading independence

d-separation, building order and naive Bayes.

d-separation: three patterns, one rule

§

A path is active if every chain and common-cause on it is unobserved, and every collider on it is observed (or has an observed descendant). Two variables are d-separated if no active path connects them.

Reading a network: two worked questions

Question 1

In Figure 9.3, are Burglary and Earthquake independent?

Yes. They are roots with no path between them other than through the collider Alarm. Since Alarm is unobserved, the path is blocked.

Are they independent given Alarm?

No. Conditioning on the collider opens the path. If the alarm sounded and there was no earthquake, a burglary becomes more likely — explaining away.

Question 2

Are JohnCalls and MaryCalls independent?

No. The path JohnCalls ← Alarm → MaryCalls is a common cause with Alarm unobserved, so it is active. A call from John makes an alarm more likely, which makes a call from Mary more likely.

Given Alarm?

Yes. Conditioning on the common cause blocks the path.

§

Both answers are read from the graph, with no arithmetic. That is the point of a graphical model: independence is a structural property, not a numerical accident.

Order matters when you build the network

A network is a factorization of the joint, and the factorization depends on the variable ordering you choose. A causal order gives a sparse network; a reversed order gives a dense one.

Same five variables, three orderings.
OrderParents
Causal: B, E, A, J, M0, 0, 2, 1, 1 → 10 numbers
Reversed: J, M, A, B, E0, 1, 2, 1, 2 → 13 numbers
Worst: M, J, E, B, A0, 1, 2, 3, 4 → 31 numbers

Why causal order wins

In a causal ordering, a variable's parents are its direct causes, and most things have few direct causes. In a reversed ordering, each variable's parents are its effects, and an effect typically has many.

This is a practical recipe: order by causation, not by convenience, even when you only care about correlation.

§

A wrong ordering does not make the model incorrect — it makes it exponentially more expensive for the same distribution.

Naive Bayes is a Bayesian network with a strong assumption

One class variable C, many features Xi, and an edge from C to every feature — and no edges between features.

P(C | x₁…xn) ∝ P(C) ∏i P(xi | C)

The graph encodes the assumption: features are conditionally independent given the class. That is almost always false — words in a document are not independent — and the classifier still works remarkably well.

Why it survives a false assumption

Classification only needs the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. Overconfident but consistently ordered scores still pick the correct class.

This is a recurring lesson: a model can be wrong about probabilities and still be useful for decisions — and it can be right about probabilities and still be harmful if you use them as if they were calibrated.

Module 11 makes the distinction precise: discrimination versus calibration.

Key takeaways

  3. 3
    Conditional independence is the modelling content.
