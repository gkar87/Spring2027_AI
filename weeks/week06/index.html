Decisions under uncertainty

From plans to policies: the MDP and the Markov property.

Why we need probability in the decision, not after it

In module 2 we assumed actions have single outcomes. Real actions do not: a robot's wheel slips, a treatment works for some patients, a delivery arrives late.

The naive fix is to plan for the most likely outcome. That is wrong whenever the unlikely outcome is catastrophic — a plan that is 95% likely to succeed and 5% likely to destroy the payload has a very different value from one that always returns safely.

The distinction that matters

Uncertainty is not ignorance. A coin is uncertain and we know its distribution exactly. An MDP encodes what we know about the distribution; it does not require us to be uninformed.

Confusing the two leads to either false confidence or unnecessary paralysis.

The definition

Markov decision process

A tuple (S, A, T, R, γ): states, actions, a transition distribution T(s′ | s, a), a reward function, and a discount factor γ ∈ [0, 1).

What each piece means

  • S — everything that affects future rewards. Nothing else is allowed in.
  • A — the choices available in each state; may differ by state.
  • T — the dynamics; usually the hard part to obtain.
  • R — a scalar per transition; the only place preferences enter.
  • γ — how much the future counts relative to now.

The reward is the specification

An MDP does not know what you want. It knows what you wrote down. Every failure of an RL system to do the right thing is, at root, a mismatch between the reward and the intention.

This is why reward design is a research topic and not a detail — module 7 returns to it.

The Markov property is a claim about sufficiency

P(St+1 | St, At) = P(St+1 | S1, …, St, A1, …, At)

The current state makes the history irrelevant. This is not a claim that the past does not matter — it is a claim that the past is summarized in the present state.

If your state does not summarize the past, the process is not Markov, and value iteration will converge to the wrong answer — quietly.

When it fails, and what to do

  • Velocity missing from a robot's state — add it.
  • Time or deadline matters — add a clock to the state.
  • Hidden variables — you have a POMDP (slide 17).
  • Long histories matter — use a frame stack, which is the Atari trick.
§

"Make it Markov" almost always means add to the state, which multiplies the state space. That trade is the central engineering tension of this module.

Return, discounting, and why γ is not a detail

Gt = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Rt+3 + ⋯ the discounted sum of rewards from time t onward

A policy π maps states to actions (or to distributions over actions). The value of a state under a policy is the expected return:

Vπ(s) = Eπ[ Gt | St = s ]

Three reasons for γ < 1

  1. Convergence. The sum is finite even on infinite horizons, so values are well defined.
  2. Preference. Earlier reward is worth more, which matches most economic and biological behaviour.
  3. Uncertainty. A reward far in the future is less likely to be collected.

The Bellman equations

One equation for a fixed policy, one for the best policy.

The Bellman expectation equation

The value of a state under a fixed policy decomposes into the immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you land:

Vπ(s) = Σs′ T(s′|s, π(s)) ·
[ R(s, π(s), s′) + γVπ(s′) ]

This is a linear system in |S| unknowns. For a fixed policy it can be solved exactly by matrix inversion — and that is exactly what policy evaluation does.

Why this is the key move

The equation converts an infinite sum over time into a relationship between neighbouring states. It is the same trick as a recursive definition, and it is what makes dynamic programming possible at all.

Everything else in this module is a way of solving this equation — exactly, iteratively, or approximately.

The optimality equation, and the two algorithms it implies

V*(s) = maxa Σs′ T(s′|s,a) · [ R(s,a,s′) + γV*(s′) ]

The only change from the previous slide is max instead of a fixed action. That single change makes the equation nonlinear, so matrix inversion no longer applies.

Two ways forward: iterate the equation until it stops changing (value iteration), or alternate evaluation and greedy improvement (policy iteration).

Solving and using MDPs

Value iteration, policy iteration, and when the state is hidden.

Value iteration: apply the equation until nothing changes

function VALUE-ITERATION(mdp, ε):
  V = {s: 0 for s in mdp.states}

  repeat:
    Δ = 0
    for s in mdp.states:
      v = V[s]
      V[s] = max over a of
               Σ T(s′|s,a) · (R + γ · V[s′])
      Δ = max(Δ, |v − V[s]|)
  until Δ < ε(1−γ)/(2γ)

  return V, greedy-policy(V)

Why it converges

Define the Bellman operator B. For any two value functions,

‖BV − BU‖∞ ≤ γ‖V − U‖∞

B is a γ-contraction in the max norm, so it has a unique fixed point, and iterating from any starting point reaches it. With γ < 1 the error shrinks by a factor of γ per sweep.

The stopping rule is not arbitrary

Stopping at Δ < ε(1−γ)/(2γ) guarantees the resulting policy is within ε of optimal. Stopping at "Δ looks small" guarantees nothing.

Worked value iteration: two states and a slippery action

States A and B, plus a terminal T. From A, the only action moves to B with probability 1, reward 0. From B, the action reaches T with probability 0.8 (reward +1), and stays in B with probability 0.2 (reward 0). Let γ = 0.5 and initialize all values at 0.

Check it by hand

In the limit, B satisfies its own equation:

V(B) = 0.8(1 + 0.5·0) + 0.2(0 + 0.5·V(B))
V(B) = 0.8 + 0.1·V(B) ⟹ V(B) = 0.8/0.9 = 0.888…

And A is one deterministic step away: V(A) = 0.5 · V(B) = 0.444…

Two things to notice

  • Information propagates backward one state per sweep. Long chains converge slowly.
  • The 0.2 self-loop raises the value of B, because staying there is not a loss — it is another chance.

Policy iteration: evaluate, then improve, repeat

  1. 1
    Initialize

    Start with any policy, even a bad one.

  2. 2
    Evaluate

    Solve the linear system for Vπ exactly.

  3. 3
    Improve

    Set π′(s) = argmaxa Σ T(s′|s,a)[R + γVπ(s′)].

  4. 4
    Stop

    When π′ = π, the policy is optimal.

Policy improvement theorem

If π′ is greedy with respect to Vπ, then Vπ′(s) ≥ Vπ(s) for all states s, with strict inequality somewhere unless π is already optimal.

Because there are finitely many deterministic policies, monotone improvement must terminate — and it terminates at a policy that satisfies the optimality equation, hence at an optimal policy.

No ε, no tolerance, no approximation: policy iteration either stops at the exact optimum or does not stop.

Modified policy iteration sits between them: evaluate with a few sweeps instead of solving exactly. In practice this is usually the right answer, and it is what many libraries implement without saying so.

A decision you could actually face

A graduate program has one server for student projects. Each week it can either upgrade (costs 2 units, reduces failure probability for three weeks) or wait (free, but a failure costs 10 units).

States: weeks-since-upgrade (0–3) and server health. Actions: upgrade, wait. Transitions: failure probability depends on both. Reward: negative cost.

What the MDP buys you

  • A policy per state, not a fixed schedule — so the timing of the upgrade adapts.
  • An explicit value of the trade: 2 units now versus 10 units × failure probability.
  • A way to test a proposed policy before deploying it, by evaluating it offline.
§

This is what "AI for operations" actually looks like: a small, well-specified MDP, solved exactly, with the value of the solution stated in the same units as the budget.

When the state is not visible, an MDP is not enough

If the agent cannot observe the state, it must act on a belief — a distribution over states. The correct formalism is a partially observable MDP.

  • The belief is updated by Bayes' rule after each observation.
  • The belief space is continuous, even when the state space is discrete.
  • Optimal policies can be arbitrarily complex; exact solutions are intractable in general.

Why this matters practically

Most deployed systems are partially observable: a support chatbot does not know the user's actual problem, a doctor does not know the true disease.

The usual engineering move is to treat the belief as the state and use approximate methods — or to pretend the problem is an MDP and accept the error. Module 10 gives you the belief update, and lab 4 makes you implement it.

§

A POMDP is not a harder MDP. It is a different problem class, and the difference is informational: actions change what you will know, not only where you will be.

Key takeaways

  1. 1
    An MDP is a specification, and the reward is the specification.

    Everything the agent does follows from what you wrote down.

  2. 2
    Markov means the state summarizes the past.

    If it does not, add to the state — and pay in state-space size.

  3. 3
    Bellman turns an infinite sum into a local equation.

    That is what makes the problem solvable at all.

  4. 4
    γ is a contraction factor, not a footnote.

    It guarantees convergence and sets the planning horizon at about 1/(1−γ).

  5. 5
    Partial observability changes the problem class.

    Beliefs, not states, and actions that gather information.
