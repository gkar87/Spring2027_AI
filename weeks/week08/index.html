Sample questions

One from each of parts B, C and D.

Sample question, part B: expansion order

Question (10 points)

The graph below has start S and goal G. Edge costs are shown. Trace uniform-cost search and then A* using the heuristic values in parentheses. Give the expansion order and the final path with its cost. Break ties alphabetically.

What the grader looks for

  • The frontier written out after each expansion, not only the order.
  • Ties resolved alphabetically, consistently.
  • A* expansion order that differs from uniform cost — if it does not, you have probably not used f = g + h.
  • The final path and its cost, stated separately.

A correct answer that omits the frontier receives about half credit. The frontier is the evidence that you ran the algorithm rather than guessed the answer.

Sample question, part C: a proof you should be able to write

Question (10 points)

Let h be admissible. Prove that A* using tree search returns an optimal solution.

Expected structure:

  1. Suppose A* returns goal G₂ with cost g(G₂).
  2. Let C* be the optimal cost, and suppose for contradiction g(G₂) > C*.
  3. There is an optimal goal G₁ with a frontier ancestor n on an optimal path. Show f(n) ≤ C*.
  4. Since A* selected G₂, f(G₂) ≤ f(n).
  5. But f(G₂) = g(G₂) > C*, a contradiction.

Where marks are lost

  • Assuming the heuristic is consistent — it is only admissible here.
  • Asserting f(n) ≤ C* without using admissibility explicitly.
  • Forgetting that the argument is for tree search, and claiming it covers graph search too.
§

A proof is graded on each step being justified, not on reaching the conclusion. The conclusion is given to you in the question.

Sample question, part D: the design question

Question (20 points)

A campus facilities office wants to schedule 240 final exams into 18 rooms across five days, subject to seating capacity, instructor availability, and a rule that no student has two exams at the same time.

Formulate this as a problem, choose an algorithm from modules 1 through 7, and justify the choice. Then state two conditions under which your choice would fail.

What earns full marks

  • An explicit state, action, and constraint set — not a paragraph of prose.
  • A named algorithm with a reason tied to the structure of the problem: 240 variables with constraints is a CSP, not a 240-deep search.
  • Two honest failure conditions: dense conflict graph, conflicting hard constraints, or a requirement for optimal room utilization rather than feasibility.

There is no single right algorithm. There is a right kind of reasoning: match the structure of the problem, then say what would break your match.

A study plan for one week

  1. 1
    Monday — reconstruct, don't reread

    From memory, write the frontier discipline, complexity, and optimality condition for all six search algorithms. Check against your notes; mark what you missed.

  2. 2
    Tuesday — traces

    Run one UCS, one A*, one minimax with pruning, one value-iteration sweep by hand on small graphs. Bring questions to office hours, 12:00–3:00 p.m., MIC D159.

  3. 3
    Wednesday — proofs and design, then rest Thursday

    Write the admissibility proof twice without looking. Then sit the practice midterm, timed, and mark it.

The three most common failures

  • Rereading slides and calling it studying. Recognition is not recall.
  • Practising only the mechanics and ignoring the 20 points of proofs.
  • Never timing an answer, then running out of time in part D.

If you can teach the A* optimality argument to a classmate without notes, you are prepared for the hardest part of this exam.
