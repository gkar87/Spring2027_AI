Harms and fairness

Three kinds of harm, and fairness criteria that conflict.

Why an AI course ends here

We spent fourteen modules making systems that optimize a specified objective. The recurring failure is that the specification is not the intention: rewards get hacked, proxies diverge, benchmarks saturate, and distributions shift.

Ethics and safety are the discipline of asking whose objective, measured how, affecting whom — before deployment rather than after.

Three claims this module defends

  1. Most harms are ordinary engineering failures with distributional consequences.
  2. Fairness is a choice among incompatible definitions, not a property you can maximize.
  3. Accountability requires a named person and a document, not a principle.
§

None of this is optional garnish. In the United States, accessibility obligations under ADA Title II and sectoral rules already apply; in the EU, the AI Act assigns obligations by risk tier. Compliance is a design input.

Three kinds of harm

A taxonomy is useful because each kind has a different detection method.
HarmWhat it looks likeHow you would detect it
Allocative A resource is distributed unequally — a loan denied, a résumé filtered, a case not flagged for review. Compare decision rates and error rates across groups at a fixed threshold.
Quality of service The system simply works worse for some people — speech recognition error rates by dialect, face detection by skin tone. Per-subgroup performance, not aggregate accuracy.
Representational The system reinforces a stereotype — image search returning only one kind of doctor, translation defaulting to gendered roles. Audit outputs across prompts and groups; read what it generates, not just its accuracy.

The third is the one aggregate metrics cannot see at all. A model can be accurate and still produce a demeaning output, because accuracy was never measuring that.

Fairness definitions conflict, and that is a theorem

Fairness definitions conflict, and that is a theorem
CriterionStatement
Demographic parityEqual positive rate across groups
Equalized oddsEqual true-positive and false-positive rates
Predictive parityEqual precision across groups
Individual fairnessSimilar people treated similarly

When base rates differ between groups, demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity cannot all hold. This is not an implementation problem; it is arithmetic.

Why they conflict

Group A has a 10% base rate, group B has 30%. If the classifier has equal precision in both, it must flag a larger share of B. If it flags equal shares, either precision or recall must differ.

Even two can conflict: with different base rates, equalized odds and predictive parity cannot both hold unless the classifier is perfect. Choosing which criterion to give up is a policy decision that belongs to the institution, not to the data scientist alone.

§

State which criterion you chose and why. An unstated criterion is still a choice — just one nobody can review.

Privacy, explanation and governance

What documentation, risk frameworks and law ask of you.

Privacy is a property of models, not just datasets

Memorization

A model can reproduce training examples verbatim — including phone numbers and copyrighted text. Larger models memorize more.

Membership inference

An attacker can often tell whether a specific record was in the training set, which is itself a privacy harm.

Attribute inference

Inferring a sensitive attribute from correlated features — the model becomes a re-identification tool.

differential privacy: P(M(D) ∈ S) ≤ eε · P(M(D′) ∈ S)

For neighbouring datasets differing in one record, the output distribution barely changes. ε is the privacy budget: smaller is stronger, and every query spends some. This is the only formal guarantee in wide use, and it is not free — it costs accuracy.

Explainability: useful, and oversold

A saliency map shows which input features affected an output. It is genuinely useful for debugging and for building trust with a domain expert.

It is not an explanation of the model's reasoning, and it is not a justification. Two models with identical saliency can have different failure modes.

What explanations are good for

  • Finding spurious features (the model looked at the watermark, not the lesion).
  • Supporting a human review process.
  • Meeting a documentation or notice requirement.

The legal and practical turn

Regulation increasingly requires notice and contestability rather than mechanistic explanation: tell the person a system was used, and give them a way to challenge the outcome.

That is achievable. "Explain the neural network" often is not — and promising it in a policy document creates an obligation you cannot meet.

Prefer explanations that name evidence: "denied because of these three factors" beats a heat map.

Documentation is the deliverable of accountability

What a model should carry

  • Intended use and explicit non-uses.
  • Training data: source, time period, consent basis, known gaps.
  • Evaluation: metrics, subgroups, intervals, dates.
  • Limitations and failure modes observed.
  • Owner: a named role, not a team alias.
  • Review trigger: what would force a re-evaluation.

Why it works

A model card is a forcing function. Writing "intended use: triage support for adult emergency patients, not for discharge decisions" prevents a class of misuse that no accuracy number prevents.

It also creates a reviewable record when something goes wrong — which is the difference between an incident and a scandal.

§

If you cannot fill in the owner and the review trigger, the system is not deployable yet — regardless of its performance.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, in one slide

Four functions, applied across the lifecycle. This is the vocabulary US organizations increasingly use.
FunctionQuestionConcrete artifact
GovernWho is accountable, and under what policy?Named owner, risk tolerance statement, approval process
MapWhat is the context, and who is affected?Use-case description, stakeholder list, harm analysis
MeasureHow do we know it is working and failing?Test plan, subgroup metrics, red-team results
ManageWhat do we do about the risk?Mitigations, monitoring, rollback plan, incident process

The framework does not tell you what an acceptable risk is. That is a decision for the institution — but the framework forces the decision to be made explicitly and recorded, which is most of the value.

The regulatory landscape, briefly and accurately

European Union

The AI Act assigns obligations by risk tier: prohibited practices, high-risk systems with conformity assessment, and transparency duties for general-purpose models.

United States

No single AI statute. Sectoral regulation (credit, employment, health, education) plus state laws, plus the NIST framework as de facto practice.

Education specifically

ADA Title II requires WCAG 2.1 AA for web content and, since 2024, for apps and platforms. A course tool that is inaccessible is a compliance problem, not a UX preference.

§

You are not expected to be a lawyer. You are expected to know which questions to route to one, and to not ship a high-risk system while those questions are unanswered.

Safety engineering: the operational practices

  • Red-teaming — an adversarial team tries to make the system fail before users do.
  • Evaluation suites — versioned tests that must pass before deployment, like regression tests.
  • Guardrails — input and output filters, with logging when they trigger.
  • Least privilege — a model with tools can act; give it only the actions it needs.
  • Kill switch and rollback — tested, not assumed.

Incident response is part of the design

Assume an incident will occur: a harmful output, a data leak, a silent regression. Write down who is woken up, what they check first, and how the system is taken offline.

Organizations that have rehearsed this recover in hours. Those that have not spend the first day deciding who is in charge.

§

A control you have not tested is a hope. That applies to your rollback plan exactly as it applies to your test suite.

Two consequences people underrate

Labour and economic effects

The evidence is mixed and task-specific: automation displaces some tasks within occupations more than whole occupations, and it creates demand for verification and oversight work.

The honest statement is that the distributional effects depend on institutional choices — who owns the productivity gain — not on the technology alone. Anyone who tells you the outcome is determined is selling something.

Environmental cost

Training and inference consume electricity and water. Inference at scale now dominates the lifetime cost for widely used models, which makes efficiency a first-order concern rather than an optimization.

Practical implication: a smaller model that meets the accuracy requirement is an environmental decision as well as an engineering one. Report the cost alongside the benefit.

§

Both topics reward precision and punish confident narrative. Cite a number, name its source, and state its uncertainty — the same standard the rest of this course holds you to.

Synthesis

The whole course in one map, and the questions to keep asking.

The whole course in one map

Look at the bottom line. It is the same question in every module: what does this assume, and what happens when the assumption fails? If you can ask that about a system you have never seen, the course has done its job.

Ten questions to ask about any AI system

  1. What decision does it support, and what happens if it is wrong?
  2. What was it trained on, and who is missing from that data?
  3. What is the base rate, and what precision does that imply?
  4. How does it perform on the worst-served subgroup?
  5. What does it assume about the world, and has that assumption been tested?
  1. How would I know it had degraded, and how fast?
  2. Can a person contest a decision it made?
  3. Who is accountable when it fails, by name?
  4. What does it cost to run, in money and energy?
  5. What would make me turn it off?
§

Question 10 is the one that separates engineering from advocacy. If nothing would make you turn it off, you are not managing a system — you are defending one.

Key takeaways

  1. 1
    Most AI harm is ordinary engineering failure with unequal distribution.

    Aggregate metrics cannot see it; per-subgroup comparisons can.

  2. 2
    Fairness definitions are mutually incompatible.

    Choose one, state it, and let someone review the choice.

  3. 3
    Privacy is a model property.

    Memorization and inference attacks operate on the model, not the dataset.

  4. 4
    Accountability needs a name and a document.

    Model cards and the NIST functions make risk reviewable.

  5. 5
    A control you have not tested is a hope.

    Red-team, rehearse the rollback, and know what would make you stop.
