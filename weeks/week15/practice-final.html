Part A: concepts (24 points, 3 each)

Answer all eight in one to three sentences each.

  1. A1. Monte Carlo evaluation and TD(0) both estimate Vπ from experience. Say what target each one moves V(s) toward, and which target has more variance.

    Answer

    Monte Carlo moves V(s) toward the whole observed return from s to the end of the episode; TD(0) moves it toward r + γV(s′), one real reward plus its own current estimate of the next state. The Monte Carlo return sums many random rewards, so it has more variance; the TD target has less variance but is biased while V is still wrong.

  2. A2. Greedy best-first search and A* both use a heuristic. Say what each one orders the frontier by, and why only one of them is optimal with an admissible h.

    Answer

    Greedy orders by h(n) alone; A* orders by f(n) = g(n) + h(n). Greedy ignores the cost already paid, so it can reach the goal by an expensive path that looked close. A* with an admissible h never pops a goal while a node on a cheaper path still has a smaller f, because that node's f is at most the optimal cost.

  3. A3. Define the Markov blanket of a node in a Bayesian network, and say why Gibbs sampling needs only the blanket to resample a variable.

    Answer

    The blanket is the node's parents, its children and its children's other parents. Given its blanket, a node is independent of every other variable in the network, so the distribution Gibbs samples from, P(X | all other variables), equals P(X | blanket) and needs only the factors that mention X.

  4. A4. Rejection sampling and likelihood weighting both estimate P(X | e). Say what each does with the evidence, and why rejection sampling wastes most of its work when e is unlikely.

    Answer

    Rejection sampling samples every variable from the prior and throws away samples that disagree with e. Likelihood weighting fixes the evidence variables to their observed values and weights each sample by the probability of that evidence given its parents. If P(e) is 0.001, about 999 of every 1,000 rejection samples are thrown away.

  5. A5. A model that predicts hospital readmission scores 0.99 AUC on validation. Name the most likely cause, give one concrete example of it, and say how you would check.

    Answer

    Leakage: a feature carries information that is only known after the outcome, or the same patient is in both training and validation. Example: a “follow-up appointment cancelled” field recorded after readmission. Check by listing the top features and asking when each is known, and by splitting by patient and by time.

  6. A6. On a test set where 2% of cases are positive, a model has 98% accuracy. Explain why that number says almost nothing, and name the two numbers you would report instead.

    Answer

    Predicting negative for every case already gives 98% accuracy, so the model may have learned nothing. Report precision and recall (or the precision at the number of cases you can act on), which ignore the large pile of easy true negatives.

  7. A7. A decoder-only language model is trained on whole sentences at once. Say why its attention needs a causal mask during training, and what goes wrong without it.

    Answer

    Each position is trained to predict the next token, and in training the whole sentence, the answer included, is in the input. The mask sets the scores of later positions to −∞, so position t can only attend to positions up to t. Without it the model copies the next token instead of predicting it, scores a near-zero training loss, and fails at generation, where later tokens do not exist yet.

  8. A8. Distinguish covariate shift from label shift, with one example of each for a deployed spam filter. Which one can you detect without new labels?

    Answer

    Covariate shift: P(x) changes but P(y | x) does not, for example a new email client changes the formatting of every message. Label shift: P(y) changes but P(x | y) does not, for example a spam campaign doubles the share of spam. Covariate shift shows up in the inputs alone, so you can detect it by comparing feature distributions without labels; label shift shows up only indirectly, as a change in the share of predicted positives.

Part B: mechanics (36 points)

Answer all four. Show the working.

  1. B1. A Bayesian network (10 points). Storm (S) and Holiday (H) have no parents; Late (L), the bus is late, has parents S and H. A lower-case letter means the variable is true. P(s) = 0.2 and P(h) = 0.1.

    P(l | S, H)
    SHP(l | S, H)
    truetrue0.9
    truefalse0.6
    falsetrue0.5
    falsefalse0.1

    (a) How many numbers does the network need, and how many would the full joint distribution need? (2)

    (b) Compute P(l). (3)

    (c) The bus is late. Compute P(s | l). (3)

    (d) You then learn it is a holiday. Compute P(s | l, h), and name the effect in a few words. (2)

    Answer

    (a) 1 + 1 + 4 = 6. The full joint over three binary variables needs 23 − 1 = 7.

    (b) Sum the four cases P(S) P(H) P(l | S, H): 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.9 = 0.018; 0.2 × 0.9 × 0.6 = 0.108; 0.8 × 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.040; 0.8 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.072. P(l) = 0.238.

    (c) P(s, l) = 0.018 + 0.108 = 0.126, so P(s | l) = 0.126 / 0.238 = 9/17 ≈ 0.529.

    (d) P(s, l, h) = 0.018 and P(¬s, l, h) = 0.040, so P(s | l, h) = 0.018 / 0.058 = 9/29 ≈ 0.310. Explaining away: the holiday already accounts for the late bus, so the storm becomes less likely. S and H are independent until L is observed.

  2. B2. HMM filtering (8 points). A patient is Well (W) or Sick (S) each day. A well patient stays well with probability 0.8; a sick patient stays sick with probability 0.6. A cough (c) is observed with probability 0.2 when well and 0.8 when sick. On day 0, P(S) = 0.5.

    (a) Predict P(S) on day 1, before any observation. (2)

    (b) On day 1 the patient coughs. Compute P(S | c). (3)

    (c) On day 2 there is no cough. Predict, then update, to give P(S) on day 2. (3)

    Answer

    (a) 0.5 × 0.6 + 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.4.

    (b) Unnormalized: Sick 0.4 × 0.8 = 0.32; Well 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.12. P(S | c) = 0.32 / 0.44 = 8/11 ≈ 0.727.

    (c) Predict: 8/11 × 0.6 + 3/11 × 0.2 = 5.4/11 ≈ 0.491. Update with no cough (0.2 if sick, 0.8 if well): Sick 5.4 × 0.2 = 1.08; Well 5.6 × 0.8 = 4.48 (both over 11, which cancels). P(S) = 1.08 / 5.56 ≈ 0.194.

  3. B3. Evaluation and a gradient step (10 points).

    (a) A spam filter is tested on 500 emails, 50 of them spam. It flags 40, of which 30 are spam. Compute precision, recall and the false-positive rate. Lowering the threshold, it flags 60, of which 40 are spam; give the new precision and recall and say which way each moved. (4)

    (b) Logistic regression has w = [0.5, −1]. One example has x = [2, 1] and y = 1. Compute ŷ, then take one gradient step with η = 0.2, using ∂L/∂w = (ŷ − y)x. Give the new w and say whether ŷ for this example went up or down. (4)

    (c) A ReLU unit in a network has z = −0.3 on every training example. What gradient reaches its incoming weights, and what does that mean for the unit? (2)

    Answer

    (a) TP = 30, FP = 10, FN = 20, TN = 440. Precision = 30/40 = 0.75; recall = 30/50 = 0.6; FPR = 10/450 ≈ 0.022. At the lower threshold: precision 40/60 ≈ 0.667 (down), recall 40/50 = 0.8 (up).

    (b) z = 0.5 × 2 − 1 × 1 = 0, so ŷ = σ(0) = 0.5. Gradient (0.5 − 1)[2, 1] = [−1, −0.5]. New w = [0.5, −1] − 0.2 × [−1, −0.5] = [0.7, −0.9]. Now z = 1.4 − 0.9 = 0.5 > 0, so ŷ went up, toward the label 1.

    (c) The derivative of ReLU at z < 0 is 0, so no gradient reaches its weights. The unit is dead: it outputs 0 and never learns unless something else moves its inputs.

  4. B4. Masked attention by hand (8 points). One causal attention head with dk = 2 over three tokens. You compute the output for token 2, whose query is q = [1, 0]. Keys: k1 = [2, 0], k2 = [0, 2], k3 = [2, 2]. Values: v1 = [1, 0], v2 = [0, 1], v3 = [1, 1]. Use the numbers on the formula sheet.

    (a) Compute the three scaled scores q · ki / √dk. (2)

    (b) Apply the causal mask. Which score changes, and to what? (1)

    (c) Compute the attention weights and the output for token 2. (3)

    (d) Compute the output without the mask, and say in one sentence why that output could not be used to train next-token prediction. (2)

    Answer

    (a) q · k1 = 2, q · k2 = 0, q · k3 = 2. Divided by √2: 1.414, 0, 1.414.

    (b) Token 3 comes after token 2, so its score becomes −∞.

    (c) e1.414 ≈ 4.11, e0 = 1, e−∞ = 0. Weights 4.11/5.11 ≈ 0.804, 1/5.11 ≈ 0.196, 0. Output 0.804 × [1, 0] + 0.196 × [0, 1] = [0.804, 0.196].

    (d) Weights 4.11/9.22 ≈ 0.446, 1/9.22 ≈ 0.108, 0.446. Output [0.446 + 0.446, 0.108 + 0.446] = [0.892, 0.554]. It mixes in token 3, the token that position 2 is supposed to predict.

Part C: arguments (20 points, two of three)

Each is worth 10 points. If you attempt all three, your best two count.

  1. C1. Derive the forward filtering recursion f1:t+1 = α P(et+1 | Xt+1) Σxt P(Xt+1 | xt) f1:t, where f1:t = P(Xt | e1:t). Name each assumption where you use it.

    Answer
    1. Split off the newest evidence and apply Bayes' rule: P(Xt+1 | e1:t+1) = α P(et+1 | Xt+1, e1:t) P(Xt+1 | e1:t). (3)
    2. Sensor Markov assumption: given the current state, the observation is independent of the past evidence, so P(et+1 | Xt+1, e1:t) = P(et+1 | Xt+1). (2)
    3. Marginalize over the previous state: P(Xt+1 | e1:t) = Σxt P(Xt+1 | xt, e1:t) P(xt | e1:t). (3)
    4. Markov assumption: given xt, the next state does not depend on earlier evidence, so P(Xt+1 | xt, e1:t) = P(Xt+1 | xt), and the last factor is f1:t. Substituting gives the recursion. (2)

  2. C2. Prove that softmax is unchanged when the same constant c is added to every score. Use it to explain why a masked score of −∞ is safe and why implementations subtract the largest score first. Then show that if the components of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, q · k has variance dk, and say what the √dk in attention is for.

    Answer
    1. esi+c / Σj esj+c = ecesi / (ec Σj esj) = softmax(s)i. (3)
    2. A score of −∞ contributes e−∞ = 0 to the sum and gets weight 0, while the other weights still sum to 1. Subtracting the largest score changes nothing by the first step, but makes every exponent ≤ 0, so no es overflows. (3)
    3. q · k = Σi=1dk qiki. Each term has mean 0 and variance E[qi2]E[ki2] = 1, and the terms are independent, so the variances add to dk. Dividing by √dk brings the variance back to 1, so the softmax does not saturate onto one token, where its gradient is nearly 0. (4)

  3. C3. Gradient descent minimizes L(w) = ½(w − 3)2 from any starting w0. Show that wt − 3 = (1 − η)t(w0 − 3). Find every learning rate η for which it converges, and describe what happens at η = 1, η = 2 and η = 3. Then say what changes for L(w) = ½a(w − 3)2 with a > 0, and what that implies for a loss that is steep in one direction and flat in another.

    Answer
    1. ∂L/∂w = w − 3, so wt+1 = wt − η(wt − 3), and wt+1 − 3 = (1 − η)(wt − 3). Applying it t times gives the formula. (3)
    2. It converges exactly when |1 − η| < 1, that is, 0 < η < 2. At η = 1 it lands on 3 in one step; at η = 2 it jumps between w0 and 6 − w0 forever; at η = 3 the distance doubles each step and it diverges. (4)
    3. With curvature a the factor is (1 − ηa), so it needs η < 2/a. One learning rate must satisfy the steepest direction, η < 2/amax, which makes progress along the flat directions slow. (3)

Part D: design (20 points)

A regional hospital wants a model that, at discharge, flags patients likely to be readmitted within 30 days, so that nurses can call them in their first week home. Nurses can call about 50 of the 1,200 patients discharged each week. The hospital has three years of records: diagnoses, length of stay, prior admissions, medications, age, zip code and insurance type. About 12% of discharges are followed by a readmission, and the rate differs between patients on public and on private insurance.

  1. D1. Formulate (5). Define the prediction target and the features you would use, and say which available data you would refuse to use and why. Name the baseline the model has to beat.

  2. D2. Evaluate (6). Describe the split and the metric you would report, given that nurses can call only 50 patients a week, and say how you would show the result is not luck.

  3. D3. Be fair on purpose (5). The base rates differ between the insurance groups. Say which fairness criterion you would aim for, what that choice gives up, and who should make it.

  4. D4. Deploy (4). Name two things you would monitor after launch and what you would do when each goes wrong, and one item the model card must say.

    What a full-credit answer looks like

    There is no single right answer. Here is one that would earn full marks.

    • D1 Target: an unplanned readmission within 30 days of this discharge (planned returns, such as scheduled chemotherapy, excluded). Features known at discharge: diagnoses, length of stay, admissions in the past year, number of medications, age. Refuse anything recorded after discharge (leakage), and think hard about zip code, which can stand in for race and income; use it only if a test shows it adds accuracy that cannot be had another way. Baseline: the rule nurses use now, such as “two or more admissions in the past year”, and a simple logistic regression.
    • D2 Split by time: train on the first two years, test on the third, with each patient in only one side, because next year is where it will be used. Report precision at 50 a week (of the 50 called, how many would have been readmitted) and recall at 50, against the baseline. Show it is not luck with a confidence interval from bootstrapping the test weeks, and by checking that it wins in most weeks, not on average only.
    • D3 With different base rates, equal precision and equal error rates cannot all hold. One defensible choice: equal recall (true-positive rate) across insurance groups, so a patient who will be readmitted has the same chance of a call whatever their insurance. It gives up equal precision: calls in one group will more often reach patients who were fine. The hospital's clinical and patient-advocacy leaders should choose, not the modeller, because it is a decision about who gets help.
    • D4 Monitor the share of flagged patients actually readmitted, monthly: if it drops below the baseline's, retrain or fall back to the old rule. Monitor the input distribution and the share of each insurance group among the 50: if either shifts (a new coding system, a new clinic), investigate before trusting the scores. The model card must state the recall and precision for each insurance group, and that the model ranks patients for a call; it does not decide care.

    Marks go to the argument: a target that is stated precisely, an evaluation tied to the 50 calls, a fairness choice with its cost named, and monitoring with an action attached.
