Perception

Vision as inference from incomplete evidence.

Vision is an inverse problem

A camera measures light intensity on a grid. The scene — objects, depth, materials, lighting — is not directly observed. Recovering it is inference from incomplete evidence, and it is ill-posed: many scenes produce the same image.

This is why vision needs priors. A convolutional network's locality, a transformer's attention, and a dataset's statistics are all priors, and they are all assumptions about what the world is like.

Three tasks, increasing structure

  • Classification — one label per image.
  • Detection — labels plus bounding boxes.
  • Segmentation — a label per pixel.

Each adds output structure, and each adds a new way to be wrong.

Detection metrics measure agreement, not usefulness

IoU = area(box ∩ truth) / area(box ∪ truth)

A detection counts as correct when its IoU with a ground-truth box exceeds a threshold. Average precision summarizes the precision–recall curve across thresholds; mAP averages over classes.

What mAP does not tell you

  • Whether the missed detections are the dangerous ones.
  • Whether boxes are stable frame to frame.
  • Whether performance holds at night, in rain, or at a different camera angle.
  • Whether a downstream planner can act on the output.

All four matter more than a two-point mAP difference.

Why vision models fail in the world

Covariate shift

The input distribution changes: new camera, new lighting, new season. The label function is unchanged.

Spurious correlation

The model learned a shortcut — snow predicts a wolf, a hospital logo predicts pneumonia. It works until the shortcut is absent.

Label shift

The class balance changes: a rare defect becomes common after a process change. Accuracy collapses even though the model is unchanged.

§

The three are distinguished by what changed — inputs, the input–label relationship, or the label distribution — and each needs a different response. Diagnosing which one you have is the first step of any fix.

Robotics

Sensing, estimating, planning and acting in a loop.

Robotics is a loop, not a pipeline

Sense, plan, act — then sense again. The loop matters because the world changes while you plan, and because actions change what you will observe next.

Planning happens in configuration space: the space of robot poses, where obstacles become forbidden regions. A path in configuration space is a motion the robot can execute.

Localization is filtering, and SLAM is localization plus mapping

A robot does not know where it is. It has odometry, which drifts, and range sensors, which are noisy. Belief over pose is updated exactly as in module 10:

bel(xt) = α · P(zt | xt) · Σxt−1 P(xt | xt−1, ut) · bel(xt−1)

This is the forward recursion from module 10, with a motion model and a sensor model. The particle filter is the standard implementation.

SLAM: a chicken-and-egg problem

To localize you need a map; to build a map you need to know where you are. SLAM solves both jointly, which is why it is harder than either.

Practical systems use graph-based formulations: poses and landmarks are nodes, and constraints from observations are edges. Optimization finds the configuration that best satisfies all constraints at once.

§

Note the pattern: a hard sequential problem becomes tractable when you stop solving it sequentially. That is the same move as module 10's variable elimination.

Planning in continuous space

A* needs a discrete graph. In continuous configuration space you either discretize (a grid, which explodes in high dimensions) or sample.

  • Grid A* — optimal on the grid, expensive in 6+ dimensions.
  • RRT — grow a tree of random samples toward free space; fast, not optimal.
  • RRT* — rewires to improve, asymptotically optimal.
  • Trajectory optimization — solve for a smooth path directly, subject to dynamics.

The dimension argument

A 7-joint arm has a 7-dimensional configuration space. A grid with 20 cells per dimension has 20⁷ ≈ 1.3 billion cells. Sampling does not care about dimension in the same way, which is why it dominates.

This is the same lesson as module 2: the state space decides which algorithm class is viable, and the answer changes with dimension.

Sim-to-real: the gap is the problem

Training in simulation is cheap, safe, and fast. Deploying a simulation-trained policy fails because simulation is wrong about friction, lighting, latency, sensor noise, and contact dynamics.

Three standard responses:

  1. Domain randomization — vary physics and appearance during training so the policy cannot rely on simulator quirks.
  2. System identification — measure the real system and match the simulator to it.
  3. Fine-tuning on real data — small amounts of real experience after simulated pretraining.

The honest framing

Sim-to-real is a distribution-shift problem, identical in structure to the vision failures earlier in this module. The "reality gap" is not mysterious; it is covariate shift in the dynamics.

Which means the diagnostic tools are the same: measure where the real distribution differs, and check performance there.

Dependable systems

Lifecycle, monitoring, uncertainty and a case study.

The lifecycle nobody teaches

  1. 1
    Frame

    What decision does this support, and what is the cost of each error?

  2. 2
    Collect

    Who is in the data, who is missing, and what consent covers it?

  3. 3
    Evaluate

    Held-out, per subgroup, with baselines and intervals.

  1. 4
    Deploy

    Shadow mode first; human review where errors are expensive.

  2. 5
    Monitor

    Inputs, outputs, and outcomes — with a rollback trigger.

  3. 6
    Retire

    Models decay; a plan for replacement is part of the design.

§

Most courses stop at step 3. Most real failures happen at steps 4 through 6 — which is where an engineer earns their salary.

A drift detector without a labelled refresh sample tells you something changed but not whether it matters. Plan the labelling pipeline before you need it.

Uncertainty: two kinds, two responses

Aleatoric

Irreducible randomness in the data — a blurry image, an ambiguous label. More data does not help. Response: report it, and design the decision to tolerate it.

Epistemic

Uncertainty from not having seen enough — an unfamiliar input. More data helps. Response: abstain, escalate to a human, or collect data there.

Why the distinction is operational

These call for opposite actions. If uncertainty is aleatoric, no amount of data will fix it, and the right move is to change the decision process. If it is epistemic, the right move is to gather data in that region.

Practical signals

  • Ensemble disagreement and MC dropout estimate epistemic uncertainty.
  • Predictive entropy mixes both, which is why it can mislead.
  • Out-of-distribution detectors target epistemic cases specifically.

Case study: automated proctoring

A system flags "suspicious behaviour" during online exams using webcam frames. It is a useful case because you have all been on the receiving end, and because every failure mode in this module appears at once.

  • Training data comes from one demographic and one lighting condition.
  • Labels come from human reviewers with their own biases.
  • The cost of a false positive is a misconduct allegation.
  • The cost of a false negative is undetected cheating.

What you would demand before deployment

  • Per-subgroup false-positive rates, not just overall accuracy.
  • A stated base rate and the resulting precision.
  • Human review of every flag before any consequence.
  • An appeal path that does not require proving innocence.
  • A published error analysis, including who was harmed.

Now apply the same list to whatever you build in your capstone. That is the assignment, in spirit.

Key takeaways

  1. 1
    Perception is inference from incomplete evidence.

    Every vision model encodes priors, and priors are assumptions.

  2. 2
    Metrics measure agreement, not usefulness.

    IoU thresholds and mAP hide the errors that matter.

  3. 3
    Robotics is a loop with latency budgets.

    Perception, planning, control, and a world that keeps moving.

  4. 4
    Distribution shift is the unifying failure.

    Pixels, dynamics, or labels — diagnose which, then fix that one.

  5. 5
    Deployment is steps 4 through 6.

    Shadow, monitor, roll back, retire. Most failures live here.
