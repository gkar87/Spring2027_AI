Exact inference

Factors and variable elimination.

The query, and why it is expensive

A query asks for the posterior of some variables given evidence:

P(Q | E = e) = α Σh P(Q, E = e, H = h)

We must sum over every assignment to the hidden variables H. That is 2|H| terms, and each term needs the full joint.

Enumeration is correct, simple, and hopeless beyond roughly 30 variables.

Two ways out

  1. Exact, cleverly — reorder the summations so shared subexpressions are computed once. This is variable elimination.
  2. Approximate, honestly — sample from the network and estimate the answer, with a confidence interval.

Both are used in practice; the second dominates for large models.

Factors: the data structure that makes it work

A factor is a table over a set of variables. The joint is a factor over all variables; a conditional probability table is a factor over a node and its parents.

Two operations are enough.

Variable elimination: sum early, sum small

Instead of enumerating all hidden variables, eliminate them one at a time, multiplying only the factors that mention the variable being removed. 

factors = [P(B), P(E), P(A|B,E), P(J|A), P(M|A)]

for var in order:            # hidden: E, A
  subset = factors mentioning var
  new    = SUM-OUT(var, PRODUCT(subset))
  factors = (factors − subset) + [new]

result = PRODUCT(factors)

The answer is identical to enumeration. The work is not: factors stay small because each one mentions only a few variables.

Why order matters

Eliminating a variable creates a new factor over its neighbours. That factor can be large. Choosing an order that keeps intermediate factors small is the entire optimization problem — and it is NP-hard in general.

§

A good heuristic: eliminate variables with the fewest neighbours first, which is the same min-degree idea as module 4's MRV.

Worked elimination, one step at a time

Query P(B | j, m) on the alarm network. Evidence is already applied: P(j|A) and P(m|A) are reduced to two-row factors over A.

  1. Eliminate E: multiply P(E) by P(A|B,E), sum over E → f₁(A,B).
  2. Eliminate A: multiply f₁(A,B) × P(j|A) × P(m|A), sum over A → f₂(B).
  3. Multiply P(B) × f₂(B) → two numbers, one per value of B. B is the query, so it is kept, never summed out.
  4. Normalize: divide by the sum.

What to notice

  • No new factor mentions more than two variables.
  • Steps 3 and 4 produce the posterior directly, with a hidden constant α.
  • The intermediate factors are not probabilities; only the normalized result is.
§

Unnormalized factors are fine. Normalize at the end, once — normalizing early loses precision and gains nothing.

The in-class version of this is three variables and two eliminations. Do it slowly once and it becomes routine.

The complexity is treewidth, not size

Exact inference costs O(n · dw+1), where w is the treewidth of the moralized graph — roughly, the size of the largest clique you are forced to build.

  • A tree has w = 1, so inference is linear.
  • A grid of size k has w ≈ k.
  • A complete graph on 30 nodes has w = 29 — hopeless.

The practical consequence

A network with 10,000 variables arranged as a tree is easy. A network with 40 densely connected variables is not. Node count tells you almost nothing; connectivity tells you everything.

This is also why the same model can be tractable for one query and intractable for another: different evidence creates different effective graphs.

Approximate inference

When exact is too slow, sample.

When exact is too slow, sample

Likelihood weighting

Generate a sample by sampling each node in topological order. When you reach an evidence variable, fix it to the observed value and multiply the sample's weight by the probability of that value.

w ← w · P(ei | parents)

The weighted fraction of samples with Q = q estimates P(q | e).

Gibbs sampling

Start with a random complete assignment consistent with the evidence. Repeatedly pick a non-evidence variable and resample it from its conditional distribution given all the others.

It needs no weights, and it often mixes better than likelihood weighting when there is a lot of evidence.

Markov chain Monte Carlo

Gibbs is an MCMC method: the sequence of assignments converges to the posterior, but successive samples are correlated, so the effective sample size is smaller than the count.

Two tools Lab 4 asks for: rejection sampling and min-fill

Rejection sampling

Sample every variable from its CPT in topological order, ignoring the evidence. Throw away each sample that disagrees with the evidence; among the rest, the fraction with Q = q estimates P(q | e).

kept ≈ N · P(e), standard error ≈ √(p(1 − p) / kept)

On the alarm network with John and Mary both calling, P(e) ≈ 0.002: of 20,000 samples about 42 survive. Likelihood weighting keeps every sample, weighted.

Choosing an order: min-fill

Eliminating a variable puts all its neighbours into one new factor, which links any two of them that were not already linked: a fill edge. Greedy min-fill eliminates next the variable that adds the fewest fill edges; a common tie-break is fewest neighbours.

Cheap, not optimal

Finding the best order is NP-hard. Min-fill is a fast greedy guess that is usually good; Lab 4 compares it with the order you give on the command line.

"Approximate" does not mean "unreliable". A sampling estimate with a reported confidence interval is more honest than an exact answer computed with a wrong model — and the second happens far more often.

Reasoning over time

The same network, unrolled, and the filters that run on it.

Reasoning over time: the same network, unrolled

A temporal model has a state Xt and an observation Et at each time step. Two assumptions make it tractable:

  • Markov — Xt depends only on Xt−1.
  • Stationary — the transition and observation models do not change with time.

The result is a hidden Markov model: one transition table, one observation table, reused at every step.

Four questions, four algorithms

Four questions, four algorithms
TaskQuestionMethod
Filtering What state am I in now, given all evidence so far? Forward recursion: P(Xt | e1:t)
Prediction Where will I be later, given evidence so far? Project forward without new observations
Smoothing Where was I, given everything including the future? Forward–backward: P(Xk | e1:t)
Most likely sequence What single path explains the observations best? Viterbi: max instead of sum
§

Viterbi and filtering look almost identical. Viterbi replaces the sum with a max, so it propagates the single best path rather than a distribution — and it keeps backpointers to recover that path.

The forward recursion, in one line of algebra

f1:t+1 = α · P(et+1 | Xt+1) · Σxt P(Xt+1 | xt) · f1:t

Read it right to left: take what you believed, project it through the transition model, weight it by how well each new state explains the observation, normalize.

That is the whole of filtering. Every Kalman filter, particle filter, and HMM forward pass is this equation with a different representation of the belief.

Worked step — a two-state world

Belief at t: [0.6, 0.4]. Transition matrix [[0.7, 0.3], [0.3, 0.7]]. Observation likelihood for e: [0.9, 0.2].

predicted = 0.7(0.6) + 0.3(0.4) = 0.54
other = 0.3(0.6) + 0.7(0.4) = 0.46

weighted = [0.54 × 0.9, 0.46 × 0.2] = [0.486, 0.092]
normalized = [0.841, 0.159]

Degeneracy

After a few steps, one particle carries almost all the weight and the rest are irrelevant. The estimate stops improving no matter how long you run.

Symptoms: all weights but one are near zero. Fixes: more particles, resampling more often, or a better proposal distribution.

§

In high dimensions, particle filters need exponentially many particles. They are a low-dimensional tool, and using them in 50 dimensions is a common and expensive mistake.

Where this is used, and what it costs

Robot localization

A particle filter over pose, updated by odometry and range sensors. The classic application, still the workhorse.

Speech and language

HMMs were the backbone of speech recognition for decades; Viterbi decoding is still in every phone.

Tracking and forecasting

Kalman filters for linear-Gaussian state, particle filters otherwise, in navigation, finance, and epidemiology.

All three share a structure: a hidden state you cannot see, observations you can, and a model of how one becomes the other. Recognising that structure is more valuable than memorising any particular filter.

§

The model is the hard part. A particle filter with a wrong transition model produces confident, precise, wrong beliefs — and a plot that looks perfectly healthy.

Key takeaways

  1. 1
    Inference is summation, and summation is expensive.

    2|H| terms if you enumerate naively.

  2. 2
    Elimination reorders the sum to keep factors small.

    Cost is exponential in treewidth, not in node count.

  3. 3
    Sampling trades exactness for scale.

    Report the error; 1/√N is slow but honest.

  4. 4
    Time is just another variable.

    Filtering, smoothing, and Viterbi are the same recursion with sum or max.

  5. 5
    Particles are for low dimensions.

    Degeneracy is not a bug you can tune away.
