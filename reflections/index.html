Reading reflections

Eight short pieces of writing, each due the Sunday that closes its week. Twelve percent of the course, and the cheapest place to find out whether you have actually understood the reading.

Dr. Gorkem Kar Eight submissions · 1.5% each · 12% total Due Sunday, 11:59 p.m. of the week named Individual

What a reflection is

Four hundred words on the week's reading. Write it before the Friday class if you can — that is what the discussion is for — and submit it by Sunday, 11:59 p.m. of the same week. Not a summary — a summary proves you opened the chapter, which is not the same as reading it. A reflection does three things, in this order:

  1. Restate the argument. In your own words, what does the reading claim, and what is it claiming against? Two or three sentences. If you cannot name what it argues with, you have not found the argument.
  2. Test it against a case. Take one situation — from your work, a system you use, a paper you have read, or a concrete example you construct — and say whether the argument holds there. This is the part that earns the marks.
  3. Name what would change your mind. One sentence: what evidence, result, or counterexample would make you drop the claim? "Nothing" is not an answer; every claim in this course could in principle be wrong.

The test of a good reflection: a classmate who read the same chapter should learn something from yours. If it only restates the chapter, it earns half marks however well written it is.

An example of the pattern

One paragraph of a reflection on the week-3 reading, to show the third part in action. Not a model to copy — the case is the writer's, and yours should be too.

The chapter argues that admissibility is the property that makes A* optimal, and it proves this for tree search while treating graph search as an implementation detail. My case is a routing service I worked on last summer, where we kept a closed set and used a heuristic fitted to historical drive times. The fitted heuristic never overestimated the true drive time on any route we checked, so as far as we knew it was admissible, but it violated consistency on about one edge in forty where a new bridge had opened. We saw occasional routes that were worse than the previous day's, which we blamed on traffic. What would change my mind here is a demonstration that consistency violations are rare enough, or small enough, that the suboptimality bound stays below the noise in the data — I have not seen one, and I no longer believe it.

How reflections are marked

Each reflection is worth 1.5%, and the case carries 45% of that.
CriterionWeightA strong submissionA weak one
Restatement 30% The claim and its target are stated in the writer's own words, and the target is named Summarises the chapter section by section
The case 45% A specific situation, described concretely enough to disagree with, and a verdict on whether the claim holds there A generic example ("in self-driving cars…")
Falsification 15% One sentence naming evidence that would change the writer's mind "Nothing — the chapter is convincing"
Precision 10% Terms used correctly; no claim stronger than the chapter supports Confuses admissibility with consistency, optimality with completeness

Reflections are marked out of four levels rather than finely: full marks, strong, adequate, and not yet. The difference between the top two is almost always the case — whether the situation is specific enough that I could say "no, that is not what happens there".

Rules

Four hundred words, ±40. Submit as text or PDF by 11:59 p.m. Sunday of the week; late reflections lose 10% per day for up to three days, after which they are not accepted at all, because their purpose is to prepare you for a discussion that has already happened.

Discussion is welcome — talk about the reading with classmates before you write. The writing must be yours. AI assistants may be used to check your understanding or to find a paper you half-remember; they may not write the reflection or supply the case. If a reflection reads as though no one who was in the room wrote it, we will talk about it.

The online section submits the same reflections on the same nights and posts one question about the recorded lecture on the discussion board in place of speaking. That question counts toward participation, not toward the reflection.

Check yourself before submitting

  • Could a classmate who read the chapter learn something from this?
  • Is your case specific enough that someone could disagree with your verdict on it?
  • Did you name what the reading argues against, not just what it argues?
  • Have you used "admissible", "consistent", "complete" and "optimal" correctly?
  • Is there one sentence that says what would change your mind?
