What to write

Not a summary. A summary proves you opened the reading, which is not the same as understanding it. Write three parts, in this order, as three paragraphs. You may give each a short heading.

  1. Restate the argument (about 100 words). In your own words, what does the reading claim that bears on the question, and what is it arguing against? If you cannot name what it argues with, you have not found the argument yet.
  2. Test it against a case (about 200 words). Take one specific situation: from your work, a system you use, a paper you have read, or a concrete example you build yourself. Describe it concretely enough that someone could disagree with you, and say whether the argument holds there. This part carries the most points.
  3. Say what would change your mind (one to three sentences). What evidence, result or counterexample would make you drop your answer? “Nothing” is not an answer; every claim in this course could in principle be wrong.

The test of a good reflection: a classmate who read the same pages should learn something from yours. One that only restates the reading earns at most half the points, however well written it is.

An example of the case and the last part

Part of a reflection on reading 3. It shows the case and the last sentence at the right level of detail. Do not copy its case; yours must be your own.

My case is a routing service I worked on last summer, where we kept a closed set and used a heuristic fitted to historical drive times. The fitted heuristic never overestimated the true drive time on any route we checked, so as far as we knew it was admissible, but it violated consistency on about one edge in forty where a new bridge had opened. We saw occasional routes that were worse than the previous day's, which we blamed on traffic. What would change my mind is a demonstration that consistency violations are rare enough, or small enough, that the suboptimality stays below the noise in the data. I have not seen one.

What to submit

  • Where: the Brightspace assignment named Reflection 1, Reflection 2 and so on. Both sections submit to the same assignment.
  • File: one PDF (preferred) or Word file named reflectionN_lastname_firstname.pdf.
  • At the top: your name, the reflection number and your word count.
  • Length: 360 to 440 words, not counting the top lines and the references. Under 360 or over 440 loses 2 of the 20 points.
  • References: point to the reading by section or page, for example “(AIMA 3.5.2)”. List anything else you used at the end, in any consistent style.
  • AI use: one line at the end saying whether you used an AI assistant and for what, as the syllabus asks. AI may help you check your understanding or find a paper; it may not write the reflection or supply the case.

How reflections are graded

Each reflection is out of 20 points and worth 1.25% of the course grade. The case carries 9 of the 20.

The difference between a strong and a full-marks reflection is almost always the case: whether the situation is specific enough that I could say “no, that is not what happens there.”

Late work and help

The course late policy applies: a late reflection loses 10% per day for up to three days and is not accepted after that. If something makes a deadline impossible, write to me before it passes.

You may talk about the reading with classmates before you write. The writing must be your own.

Check before you submit

  • Does it answer the question in the table, about the reading in the table?
  • Is it 360 to 440 words, with your name, the reflection number and the word count at the top?
  • Did you name what the reading argues against, not just what it argues?
  • Is your case specific enough that someone could disagree with your verdict on it?
  • Is there a sentence that says what would change your mind?
  • Did you add the one-line AI note?
